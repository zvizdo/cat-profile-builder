Use this whenever you're writing bio text from nothing, rewriting it, or shortening it — the
"Write a bio" chip, a request to rewrite, or a step inside `build-profile`.

## Before you write: look, then ask only if you need to

This section is for a bio asked for on its own — the "Write a bio" chip, or the volunteer
typing a request to write one. Skip it entirely in two cases:

- **A request to rewrite or shorten** an existing bio. The material is already on the page;
  go straight to the writing. The source rules below still apply.
- **Inside `build-profile`.** The build's own interview has already happened and the answers
  are in this conversation; go straight to the writing. The source rules below still apply.

Otherwise:

1. **Look first, in as few steps as you can.** If you haven't read the outline in this
   request yet, do. Then call `read_page` and `list_media` in the same step — `read_page`
   gives you the facts and every section's words at once (the bio, captions, the day scenes,
   a quote, needs cards). Then `view_photos` on up to six of the most telling photos: the cat
   with people or other animals, doing something specific, in a place that says something
   about her. Skip near-duplicates.
2. **Decide whether you have enough.** You have enough when you know both:
   - at least two concrete things she does — a habit, a game, how she greets people, where she
     naps; and
   - at least one fact about the home she'd suit — how she is with other cats, dogs or kids,
     her energy, or a medical or care need.

   If you have both, write straight away. Don't ask anything.
3. **If you don't, ask one to three questions, one per message**, and wait for each answer
   before the next. Ask only about what's missing.
   Each question should point to something specific you saw, whenever there is something to
   point to — "I can see her curled up on a windowsill in two of the photos. Is that her
   favourite spot, and what does she watch out there?" beats "What does she like to do?"
   Stop asking as soon as you have enough. The moment the volunteer says anything like
   "just write it", stop and write with what you have.

   When the volunteer answers, don't look again — what you read and saw is already in this
   conversation. Ask the next question, or write. An answer of "I don't know" settles that gap
   — don't ask about it again.

## Every sentence has a source

This applies to every bio you write — on its own, a rewrite or a shortening, or
inside `build-profile` — even when you skipped the section above.

An "I don't know" leaves that topic out of the bio. An unknown is not a "hasn't," a "never,"
or a "yet": "I don't know about dogs" gives you nothing to say about dogs.

**Photos show; they don't prove.** What a photo plainly shows — her coat, a dog in the frame,
a windowsill — you can use. What it only suggests — that she loves the window, that she gets
on with the dog — is a guess, and you must never write a guess from a photo as fact.
Ask it as a question, and write it only once the volunteer confirms it.

This holds for every sentence you write, including after "just write it". From a photo alone
you may say where she is and what she's doing in that frame — "she's stretched along the back
of the sofa." You may not add when, how often, why, or how she feels about it — "every
morning," "once the house goes quiet," "she loves the view," "calm and observant," "all she
needs." Nor may you stitch a photo into something the volunteer told you, as if it came next
in her day — "after the birds leave, she naps on the sill" invents a sequence nobody gave you.
Those come only from the page or from the volunteer's own words, and even then say no
more than those words say: "no dogs" is not "no other pets," and "head-butts your hand" is not
"wants chin scratches." Before you save, check each sentence against its source — a photo, the
page, or an answer — and cut or pull back any that says more than its source does. If the page
doesn't give her sex, don't guess it from a photo: write "they." A shorter bio that is true,
even under 80 words, beats a fuller one that guesses.

## Voice

Plain, specific, never cute. Describe the cat by what she does, not by adjectives stacked on
top of each other. "A negotiator, not a complainer" tells a reader something; "sweet, loving,
playful, affectionate" tells them nothing, because it could describe any cat on the page. One
concrete, observed detail per sentence beats a paragraph of description. Say what you've seen
her do, not what you promise she'll do for someone else — "she's gotten along well with every
cat she's met" holds up; "she'll get along with your cat" doesn't, because nobody can promise
that. No emoji.

## Opening

Start with something she does, not a fact sheet. Age and sex already live on the page as
separate facts — don't repeat them in the first sentence. There's no separate field for breed
or coat colouring, so if it's worth mentioning, work it into the bio itself as a concrete
detail rather than leaving it out. A good opening line puts the reader in a small, specific
moment: what she does at the window, what she does when you sit down, what she does with a
toy.

An opening built from her coat colour or eye colour is still a fact sheet, even when it's
phrased as if it were a moment — "she stops people in their tracks with her fluffy grey coat
and green eyes" describes a photo, not something she does. Put her in an action instead: what
she was doing when that coat or those eyes were worth noticing.

## Length and paragraphing

80–160 words when the material is there; when it's thin, stop short rather than pad (see
"Every sentence has a source" above). One paragraph, or two short ones if the material
genuinely splits — for instance, personality in the first, home needs in the second. Longer than that starts losing
readers before they reach the honest, important part.

## What a visitor wants to know

Somewhere in the bio, touch what you actually know among: age (lightly, if it matters to the
story), how she is with other cats or dogs, whether she's good with kids, anything notable
about litter habits, her energy level, and any medical or behavioural need. Skip whatever you
don't know rather than guessing or padding. Weave these in as behaviour, not a checklist —
"she'd rather nap through a busy house than referee it" says quiet-household better than
"good with: calm homes only."

## Honest about needs

State a real limitation plainly, in the present tense, and frame it forward rather than as a
warning label. "She doesn't love sharing the spotlight with other cats" carries the same fact
as "cat aggressive" without sounding like a diagnosis. Never soften a need into vagueness —
say what it actually looks like day to day — and never drop it to make the bio sound rosier.

## What never to write

- "Sweet boy/girl looking for his/her forever home," or any close cousin of it.
- Adjectives with nothing behind them — if a claim has no observed detail backing it, cut the
  claim or find the detail.
- A hard-luck or sad-story opening. People rarely adopt out of pity, and it buries who she is
  now under what happened to her before.
- Jargon a first-time adopter won't parse ("resource guarding," "decompression period")
  without translating it into what it looks like.
- Guarantees about future behaviour in someone else's home.
- Exclusionary phrasing about who else can be in the house ("only dog," "no children") —
  say what she needs instead, the way "Honest about needs" above describes.
- Gatekeeping language ("qualified adopters only").

## Shortening without losing the concrete details

When asked to shorten: cut adjectives and throat-clearing first, then merge sentences that
share a subject. Keep the one concrete opening detail and the honest-needs sentence — those
are the two things worth the word count. If a sentence has no specific, observed detail in
it, it's the first one to go.

Once the text is set, `read_blocks` on the bio block to re-read what you changed and confirm
what actually saved.
