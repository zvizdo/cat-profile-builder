# Research: what makes an adoption bio work

Read for T033, to ground the four helper skills (`src/core/helper/skills/*.md`) in what
shelter-marketing practice actually recommends, not house style alone.

## What works

- **Behaviour over adjectives.** "He has gotten along well with all the dogs he's met" beats
  "friendly." Say what was observed, not what is promised — nobody can guarantee future
  behaviour, and observed fact reads as more credible than a claim (ASPCApro, Best Friends).
- **One concrete opening detail, not a stat block.** Lead with something the cat does —
  Best Friends' example: "You haven't lived until you've had Sammy's nose on your lap" — not
  age, breed, or intake number. This app's fact strip only holds age and sex (breed and
  coat colour have no field of their own), so the opening line is exactly where a detail like
  that belongs if it's worth keeping at all.
- **Plain words for behaviour terms.** "Selective," "reactive," and "high energy" are shelter
  jargon the public doesn't reliably parse and sometimes misreads as worse than intended.
  HeARTs Speak's fix: replace the label with what it looks like — not "high energy" but
  "appreciates a long walk or a jog most days, and room to romp at home."
- **Honest about needs, framed forward.** State a real limitation, then say what it looks like
  day to day, in the present tense — not "damaged," not a diagnosis dump. HeARTs Speak reframes
  "dog selective" as "doesn't like sharing the spotlight with other pets" — the fact survives,
  the alarm doesn't.
- **Balance every challenge with a positive nearby**, so the challenge isn't the last thing a
  reader remembers (Best Friends).
- **Short.** Petfinder and Best Friends both favor one paragraph or two to three short ones over
  a long history. Long medical or behavioural detail can come later, in person — the bio's job
  is to get someone to ask, not to fully brief them.
- **What adopters actually ask, and the bio (or the page around it) should answer**: age,
  whether the cat gets along with other pets, whether the cat is good with kids, litter-box
  habits, energy level, and any medical or behavioural need. A bio that never touches any of
  these leaves the reader with the one question they'd have asked a volunteer anyway.
- **A close that invites contact**, not a gatekeeping line ("qualified adopters only").

## What fails

- **Sad-story framing.** Maddie's Fund is direct: people rarely adopt out of pity, and a bio
  built around a hard past reads as a fundraising appeal, not an introduction. It also buries
  the one thing that actually moves adoption — a sense of who this animal is now.
- **Adjective piles with nothing behind them.** "Sweet, loving, playful, affectionate" said four
  times about four different cats tells an adopter nothing distinguishing. Every claim needs a
  concrete detail or observed instance backing it, or it should be cut.
- **"Sweet boy/girl looking for his/her forever home."** The single most-repeated shelter
  cliché in the sources reviewed; it signals a bio nobody actually wrote, and skips every
  question above.
- **Front-loading medical/behavioural detail before any personality.** Puts the hardest
  information first, which reads as a warning label rather than an introduction, and loses
  readers before the good part.
- **Vocabulary a first-time adopter won't parse** ("resource guarding," "decompression period")
  used without translation.

## Length and structure taken into the skills

80–160 words, one to two short paragraphs, present tense, one concrete detail per sentence —
consistent across the sources above and adopted as `write-bio`'s working range.

## Published profiles reviewed

Five real, currently-listed cat adoption bios, fetched or searched directly, to check the
how-to guidance above against what actually ships.

- **[Bootsy — SPCA Florida](https://www.spcaflorida.org/adopt-a-cat)** (fetched directly).
  Opens: "Bootsy is a little bundle of personality, sass, and surprises!" Works because it's
  one sentence, no stats, and promises a specific kind of personality (playful, a little
  unpredictable) rather than a generic "sweet."
- **[Professor McGonagall — Brandywine Valley SPCA](https://bvspca.org/adopt/adopt-a-cat)**
  (fetched directly). Opens: "Meet Professor McGonagall, a uniquely independent and shy cat
  with a magical touch of extra toes!" Works because "extra toes" is one concrete, visual
  detail an adopter can picture immediately, alongside an honest temperament note
  (independent, shy) stated without apology.
- **[Avocado Roll — Brandywine Valley SPCA](https://bvspca.org/adopt/adopt-a-cat)** (fetched
  directly). Opens: "Meet Avocado Roll, a young, cuddly cat with a zest for life that's as
  refreshing as his name suggests." Weaker than the two above — "zest for life" is close to an
  adjective pile — but the concrete, playful name anchors it and it's still short.
- **[Mimi Mittens — Brandywine Valley SPCA](https://bvspca.org/adopt/adopt-a-cat)** (fetched
  directly). Opens: "Mimi Mittens is a beautiful, mostly white 8 year old lady who is in need
  of your love and gentle care." Included as a contrast case: it leads with age and colour —
  exactly the stat-block opening the how-to guides above warn against — and "in need of your
  love and gentle care" edges toward the sad-story framing Maddie's Fund flags as
  ineffective.
- **[Bassie — Petfinder](https://www.petfinder.com/cat/bassie-48e4c0ad-368f-428e-98a1-62c4cf1124c1/ny/new-york/paul-the-cat-guy-inc-ny1376/details/)**.
  Direct fetch was blocked (HTTP 403); reached only through a search-engine summary of the
  listing, which quotes: "Bassie is a sweet and adorable girl who loves being close to her
  person and will happily cuddle up beside you and gently tap you with her paw when she wants
  more pets and attention." Included anyway because the summary is specific enough to trust —
  it names a described behaviour (the paw-tap) rather than only an adjective — and because it
  also states plainly that she's fine with cats and dogs but equally fine as an only cat, a
  clean example of the "state the fact, don't gatekeep on it" pattern.

A sixth candidate, a Best Friends Animal Society individual cat page surfaced by search,
returned HTTP 404 on direct fetch — these listings rotate off as soon as a cat is adopted, so
the specific page no longer existed by the time it was fetched. Best Friends' own written
guidance (already in Sources, below) was used in its place.
